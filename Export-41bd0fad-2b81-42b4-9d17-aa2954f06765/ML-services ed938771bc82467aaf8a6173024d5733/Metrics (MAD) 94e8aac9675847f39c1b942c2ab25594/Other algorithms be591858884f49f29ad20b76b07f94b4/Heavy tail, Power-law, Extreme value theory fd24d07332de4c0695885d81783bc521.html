Literature review

There are many resources that discuss about the MAD (median absolute deviation from the median) in the context of heavy tailed distributions: [1], [2], [3], [4]. However, upon further investigation it is found that MAD is useful for identifying the core region of the data. Thus, more points from the heavy tails could be classified as anomalies when using MAD. Our goal is to reduce the number of points (in the tail) that are classified as anomalies.

  • Maybe, considerable difference between standard deviation and MAD implies that the data has a heavy tail. We could have a logic based on comparing these two quantities to decide whether the data has a heavy tail and then use a more strict threshold if a heavy tail is detected.

Among the phrases "heavy tails", "power law" and "extreme value theory", it was "extreme value theory" that led to more references regarding outlier detection with less false positives when the data follows a heavy tailed distribution.

From "Anomaly detection: A survey" by Chandola et al, 2009:

Extreme Value Theory (EVT) [Pickands 1975] is a similar concept as anomaly detection, and deals with extreme deviations of a probability distribution. EVT has been applied to risk management [McNeil 1999] as a method for modeling and measuring extreme risks. The key difference between extreme values and statistical anomalies is that extreme values are known to occur at the extremities of a probability distribution, while anomalies are more general. Anomalies can also be generated from a different distribution altogether.

One observation from many of the below papers is that a common approach is to find a threshold that can be used to classify a point as being extreme. This has been confirmed after discussing with @Isaac Cohen Sabban also. The paper "Anomaly Detection in Streams with Extreme Value Theory", Siffer et al, KDD 2017 is a somewhat gentler way to get intuition about this topic.
