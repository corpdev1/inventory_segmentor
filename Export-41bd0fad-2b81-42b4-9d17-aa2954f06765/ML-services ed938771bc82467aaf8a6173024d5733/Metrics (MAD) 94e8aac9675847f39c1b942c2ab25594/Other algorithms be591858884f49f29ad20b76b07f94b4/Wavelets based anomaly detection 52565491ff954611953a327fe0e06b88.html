Why wavelets?

  • Well studied signal representation.

    Wavelets can model both time and frequency information in a localized manner. In contrast, one needs time windowing to do the same with Fourier analysis. However, Wavelet transforms like Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) are computationally more efficient.

    In more detail, the Fourier approach will work very well when the frequency spectrum is stationary. That is, the frequencies present in the signal are not time-dependent; if a signal contains a frequency of x Hz, this frequency should be present equally anywhere in the signal. The more non-stationary/dynamic a signal is, the worse the results will be.

  • DWT transforms an original time series data into two types of wavelet coefficients at multiple levels: approximation and detail. Intuitively, approximation is obtained by averaging (aggregation) and detail is the difference of the original time-series w.r.t. the approximation.

    Anomalies are generally captured in either of these two sets of coefficients in one or more of the levels.

    Haar wavelets are good to get a quick intuition. A nice introduction.

    • At level i, the coefficients represent a window of size 2i2^i2i﻿.
  • DWT leads to a well grounded multiscale representation of the time-series.
  • Not restricted by input being stationary etc.
    • Need to check how time-series of lengths which are not powers of 2 are handled.
  • Wavelet representation occupies same amount of memory as the original time-series.

Multi-scale Anomaly Detection with Wavelets

  • Paper shows a way to compute DWT incrementally.
  • The wavelet used in the paper is the Coiflet.

    We use the “Coiflet” 6 tap wavelet, sometimes abbreviated C6, with six scaling and wavelet coefficients. The C6 wavelet has two vanishing moments: the zeroth moment, or a constant signal; and the first moment, or a straight line. In practice this means that the detail coefficients for a straight line are 0. This is desirable because in many timeseries, straight lines are typically unremarkable—consider a metric representing a counter of events, which under normal operation continues upward at a constant rate.

    More importantly, the C6 wavelet introduces almost no distortion into the phase of features in the signal, as shown by Percival and Walden. For this reason, they recommend it and other wavelets in the Coiflet family for applications, like anomaly detection, which need to maintain the locality of features.

  • Computational cost

    To compute a totally phase-invariant wavelet transform requires computing the usual transform and a version shifted by one index. Twice the computational cost of computing a wavelet transform. However, the wavelet transform itself is quite cheap to compute, they claim.

  • Anomaly detection approach and memory requirement
    • Anomaly detection based on the coefficients: both detail and approximation.
    • Coefficients from all levels and both types are considered.
    • In each case, coefficient values are processed by taking average of square root values over windows of eight samples.
    • Kernel density estimation (KDE) on the resulting values are used for anomaly detection.
      • Surprise quotient of a value: ratio of integral of the square of the probability density function and probability of given value.
    • The KDE representation is updated in a streaming manner and needs to be saved. Memory footprint of 1 MB for a time-series of 15000 samples.
