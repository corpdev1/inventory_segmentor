The main findings of all the experiments are summarized below:

Summary

  • FluxInfer adjusts each and every time series till it is either a step or a constant, with limited noise. This leads to the removal of spikes (Except if it happens exactly at the instance of anomaly. FluxInfer does not change the last part of the time series. In this case the time series would have transformed into a noisy constant plus a spike at the end.). This will influence the strength of the edges between the metrics computed based on the Fisher Z-test. Many of the signals have spiky behavior, which could actually be their true behavior and not noise. For e.g., innodb#buffer_pool#pages#misc is highly correlated w.r.t. threads#running (edge weight = 574948.29). It is most likely happening because of the spikes appearing in the thread metric (check the figures attached below), which may very well be related to the spikes in the innodb metric. The approach of FluxInfer suppresses such spikes and thus affects the weights of the graph. Also, it is not clear what is the output of the denoising operation to time series without anomalies, like the network metrics.
  • Fisher Z test captures correlation (positive and negative) reasonably well. For e.g., the negative correlation between the metrics questions and cpu#total#pct is captured well. A window of size 96 samples works as well as a window of size 340, if the new larger window does not have significant new behavior. See below for weights for some pairs, to get an intuition about how it works.
  • The paper is not mentioning where the window is considered. Whether it is centered w.r.t. the incident or whether the incident is on one side. In the section about denoising, they mention that the time series is such that the anomaly is at one end. The anomaly can happen in different metrics for different time stamps (some metrics have anomalies happening at an earlier point in time). They have not given more details in the section concerning the common time window in the Fisher Z-test. Also, the window size is not mentioned.
  • The weighted graph is made of three strongly connected components and the PageRank algorithm is giving weights to nodes based on their influence within the connected component. See below for experiment with unweighted graph.
  • The innodb metrics that are ranked highly by our FluxInfer implementation, are mainly highly correlated with each other (See below for all edge weights). Two of them are ranked highly because of the weight that PageRank is giving those nodes (0.0473, 0.04618) w.r.t. that strongly connected component.
  • The network based metrics don't exhibit any anomaly but are ranked very highly by our FluxInfer implementation (weights 0.04198, 0.03218). This is mainly because of the high correlation between the network metrics, as you can see in the figures attached below. The network metrics show low correlation with all the other metrics.
  • The weight of cpu#total#pct in the case of CPU saturation experiment, comes fourth within its strongly connected components if you round of the rank scores to four significant digits. But the scores are close. 1st: 0.03108. 2nd: 0.02853, 3rd: 0.02852, cpu#total#pct: 0.02836. Note there are many nodes with same rank score for all the scores.
  • Within a connected component, the nodes with higher total edge weights seem to rank higher.
  • In order to test the influence of the strongly connected components on the computed rank, an unweighted graph was computed. The edges where set to either weight 1 or removed based on p_value < 1e-5. Even in this case, the top rank metrics are the same. The innodb metrics and network based metrics are in separate connected components altogether and are assigned weights based on those components.
  • The approach taken by FluxInfer is based on the first step of the PC algorithm, where an undirected graph is constructed based on independence tests between pairs of variables. In the case of FluxInfer, they retain a fully connected graph and assign the scores of the Fisher Z-test to the edges.
  • The approach taken by FluxInfer is based on the first step of the PC algorithm, where an undirected graph is constructed based on independence tests between pairs of variables. Also, FluxInfer stops at the first stage of conditional independence test, where no third variable is considered. The PC algorithm conducts conditional independence tests based on the neighbours of the two variables.

    In the case of FluxInfer, they retain a fully connected graph and assign the scores of the Fisher Z-test to the edges. These edge weights interact with PageRank to give the final ranks.

Some more points to note:

  • FluxInfer only presents average performance across all the test cases. Most likely, it performed better in the Stress-ng based cases (where the root cause metric can be clearly identified) than in the case of workload spike (where many database related metrics could behave like the root cause).
  • The PC algorithm requires considerable training data to construct the DAG describing the causal relationship between variables. There is a separate inference process when an incident happens.

    FluxInfer is an algorithm that models the relationship between variables just based on information that is available around the time of the anomaly. Thus, the comparison w.r.t. PC is not fair in the FluxInfer paper.

  • Fisher Z-test is for comparing two variables which are both continuous values. For discrete values, one uses to tests like chi-square test and G-square test. In our case, there are many variables that behave like continuous valued for all practical purposes. However, we have some variables that distinctly behave like discrete variables (e.g., metrics based on number of threads).

Note: In our experiments, the MySQL performance metrics have been removed. MySQL performance metrics are made of two groups: events statements and table i/o. The latency metrics in events statements are noisy and showing no pattern. Information similar to table i/o metrics are being captured by other metrics like questions and others.

Points to discuss with @Valentin Lapparov:

In order to run MAD locally:

  • The environment variable KAFKA_BROKERS needs to be set.
  • There are two places where S3 is accessed. In one case, the except condition needs to be changed, so that the code runs smoothly locally when access to S3 is not possible. In the other case, the access to S3 should be wrapped in try-except so that the code runs smoothly when access to S3 is not possible.

In the MAD pipeline:

  • Why are the timestamps in the output csv files multiplied by 1000?
  • Are missing time stamps imputed? Some times yes. Some times no (de17-de17/mongodemo/mongodb.collstats/mongodb#collstats#insert#count/SeriesDB.series/SeriesDB.series_mongodb.collstats_mongodb#collstats#insert#count.csv).
  • csv file can have duplicate rows for same time stamp value. Is it ok? e.g., 'de17-de17/mongodemo/mongodb.status/mongodb#status#locks#collection#acquire#count#W/no_subject/no_subject_mongodb.status_mongodb#status#locks#collection#acquire#count#W.csv'
  • Right now, there is a check on the maximum length on the subject string. Why is it there?
  • To obtain System metrics, we have to run MAD on a separate topic. In the output results folder there are folders for MySQL metrics, also. The anomaly scores in these folders are different from the values that you get from the topic that you use for MySQL metrics.
  • Sometimes, the output of MAD has duplicate entries, with same timestamp and value but different anomaly scores.

Some questions

  • How big should the window be?
  • How should it be centered w.r.t. incidence of anomaly? FluxInfer doesn't give proper details.
  • Should the signals be denoised? FluxInfer's approach to denoising doesn't appear stable.
  • How to define an incident? Right now, it is based on how we define global anomaly (at least one anomalous metric). A logic to group anomalies within a window together as one anomaly was also tried but it is taking more time to run.
  • It is not yet clear how to interpret the top metrics returned by FluxInfer.

To understand further

  • mysqldump, log-flux and refresh are executed without causing anomalous incidents. It looks like these operations are being performed without locking out the client queries. People are talking about performing mysqldump after locking (at least, read-only lock). These need to be tried.
  • Restoring tables on a live stream, leads to duplicate entries in the tables. This makes these tables unusable for running queries later. Searching for restoring tables on live stream or for restoring tables safely etc, has still not led to a solution that can be implemented.
  • Currently implemented slow JOIN query is not detected by the system.

To explore further

  • Extend the approach based on other ways to rank KPIs.
